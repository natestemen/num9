About

NMBR 9 is a tile-laying game: twenty tiles, two of each number from 0 to 9, are drawn one at a time and each must be placed before the next is seen. A tile scores its number times the level it sits on (the ground is level 0), and to go up a level it must lie entirely on tiles below, on at least two of them. This site runs the num9 engine, written in Rust and compiled to WebAssembly, entirely in your browser.

Hints

Ask for the best move and the engine plays the rest of the game out hundreds of times from each candidate placement, with the tiles still to come shuffled each time (Monte Carlo rollouts), then ranks the candidates by their average final score. After a game it plays the same tiles itself, and, now that the order is known, searches for the best play of that exact deal (nested Monte Carlo search), which you can step through next to your own.

Game analysis

After a game the engine goes back over every move, the way a chess engine reviews a game. It plays the rest of the game out from the position before each move, from your move and from the alternatives, and grades yours by the expected final points it gave away: best, excellent, good, inaccuracy (?!), mistake (?) or blunder (??). That gives an accuracy for the game and for its opening, middle and end; a graph of the expected final score; a reason for each slip in words; the lessons that cost the most; and a way to retry any move to find a better one.

The highest possible score

With the order of the tiles free, the maximum score is an open problem. Lagerkvist's Nmbr9 as a Constraint Programming Challenge (2020) cites 229 as the best known. The engine designs stacks from the top down (choose what goes on the highest level, then cover it as cheaply as possible, level by level) and found 236, reaching level 6, along with 1,038 distinct plays above 200. The best are in Records.

In the other direction, no play can score more than 354. An integer program assigns tiles to levels to maximise the score, subject to facts every legal stack obeys. These include that a level is never larger than the one below it, and that anything above the ground rests on at least two tiles. The rest are shape facts computed by exhaustive search: how much wider the level below one or two tiles must be, and how much area a level of two or three tiles can hold up. So the true maximum is somewhere between 236 and 354.

Small decks, solved

With only a few tiles the whole game can be searched: every reachable position, counting stacks that differ by sliding or turning as the same. That gives the exact value of perfect play, and the most each deck can score with the order chosen freely, which the Challenges ask you to reach.

Help

How to play

Place the tiles one at a time, in the order they're drawn. Each tile scores its value × its level (the ground is level 0). A tile must touch another tile on its own level. To go up a level it must sit entirely on tiles below, on at least two. Tiles land on whatever is underneath, so just slide them around.

Reading the board

Tiles show their value and level (7² = a 7 on level 2) and get brighter the higher they sit. Tiles above the ground have a rim in their level's colour (the same colours as the Levels box), and higher tiles cast shadows on lower ones. Hover over or tap a level in the Levels box to see only its tiles; ▦ (or tab) colours the board by level. The last tile placed is underlined.
